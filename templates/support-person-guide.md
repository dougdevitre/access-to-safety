# Supporting Someone Experiencing Abuse: A Guide for Friends, Family, and Coworkers

> **AI Instructions:** When a user identifies as a support person (friend, family member,
> coworker, neighbor, faith leader) who is concerned about someone experiencing abuse or
> violence, use this guide. Walk them through sections relevant to their situation. Validate
> their concern — reaching out for guidance is an important step. Emphasize safety for BOTH
> the survivor and the support person. Never encourage them to confront the abuser. Remind
> them that the survivor is the expert on their own safety and that leaving is a process,
> not a single event. If they describe a situation involving a child in danger, pivot to
> the mandatory reporter checklist or provide the Childhelp hotline (1-800-422-4453). If
> they express concern that the person may be suicidal, provide 988 immediately.

---

## You Are Reading This Because You Care

The fact that you are looking for information on how to help someone means you are already
doing something important. Supporting someone through abuse is hard. It can feel frustrating,
scary, and helpless. This guide will help you understand what is happening, what you can do,
and how to take care of yourself through the process.

**The most important things to know:**
1. You cannot rescue someone. But you can be a lifeline.
2. The survivor is the expert on their own safety. Respect their decisions.
3. Leaving is dangerous, complex, and rarely happens on the first try. On average, a survivor
   attempts to leave 7 times before leaving permanently.
4. Your role is to provide support, information, and options — not ultimatums.

## Part 1 — Recognizing the Signs

Abuse is often hidden. The person may not tell you directly, or they may not call it abuse.
Here are patterns to watch for.

### Warning Signs of Domestic Violence

**Behavioral changes:**
- Withdrawing from friends, family, or activities they used to enjoy
- Becoming unusually anxious, jumpy, or fearful
- Making excuses for their partner's behavior
- Avoiding certain topics or becoming evasive about their home life
- Canceling plans at the last minute (especially when the partner is involved)
- Checking their phone constantly or seeming afraid of missing a call/text

**Physical signs:**
- Unexplained injuries or injuries with explanations that do not make sense
- Wearing clothing that covers arms, legs, or neck in warm weather
- Visible flinching or tensing when the partner is nearby
- Looking to the partner before speaking or answering questions

**Signs of control:**
- The partner monitors their phone, social media, or location
- The partner controls the money — they have no access to cash or accounts
- The partner speaks for them or answers questions directed at them
- They are not allowed to drive, work, or see certain people
- The partner shows up unexpectedly at places where they are

**In the workplace:**
- Frequent absences, tardiness, or leaving early without explanation
- Receiving excessive calls, texts, or visits from a partner
- Drop in work performance or concentration
- The partner showing up at the workplace uninvited
- Expressing fear about going home

### Warning Signs of Escalating Danger

These signs suggest the situation is becoming more dangerous. If you observe multiple
indicators, the person may be at high risk:

- [ ] The abuser has threatened to kill the person, the children, or themselves
- [ ] The abuser has access to weapons (especially firearms)
- [ ] The person has been strangled or choked (strangulation is the strongest predictor of future lethal violence)
- [ ] The violence is increasing in frequency or severity
- [ ] The person has recently left or is planning to leave (the most dangerous time)
- [ ] The abuser has followed, stalked, or surveilled the person
- [ ] The abuser has harmed or threatened pets
- [ ] The abuser is increasingly controlling (monitoring movement, isolating from support)
- [ ] The abuser uses drugs or alcohol (increases unpredictability)
- [ ] The abuser has a criminal history involving violence

> **If you believe someone is in immediate danger, call 911.**
> You do not need the survivor's permission to call for help in an emergency.

## Part 2 — How to Talk to Someone You Are Worried About

### What to Say

Choose a private, safe moment when the abuser is not present or listening.

**Opening the conversation:**
- *"I've noticed some things that worry me, and I care about you. Can we talk?"*
- *"I'm here for you, no matter what. I want you to know that."*
- *"I saw [specific thing], and I want to make sure you're okay."*

**If they disclose:**
- *"I believe you."*
- *"This is not your fault."*
- *"You don't deserve this."*
- *"I'm here for you, whenever you're ready."*
- *"What do you need right now?"*
- *"You're not alone in this."*

**If they are not ready to talk:**
- *"I understand. I'm not going to push. But I want you to know I'm here whenever you want to talk."*
- *"There's no timeline on this. I'm not going anywhere."*

### What NOT to Say

| Avoid saying... | Why it hurts |
|----------------|-------------|
| *"Why don't you just leave?"* | Leaving is dangerous, complex, and may not be possible right now. This question implies the solution is simple and they are choosing to stay. |
| *"I would never put up with that."* | This implies blame — that they are weak for staying. Abuse can happen to anyone. |
| *"You need to call the police."* | The survivor knows their situation best. Police involvement can escalate danger in some cases. Offer it as an option, not a demand. |
| *"What did you do to make them angry?"* | Nothing justifies abuse. This question shifts blame to the victim. |
| *"I'm going to go talk to them."* | Confronting the abuser can trigger retaliation against the survivor. Never do this. |
| *"You need to think about the kids."* | They ARE thinking about the kids. Survivors often stay to protect their children. This adds guilt. |
| *"At least they didn't..."* | Minimizing abuse invalidates their experience. All abuse is serious. |
| *"If you don't leave, I can't help you."* | Ultimatums isolate the survivor further — exactly what the abuser wants. |

### If They Say "It's Not That Bad" or Deny Abuse

This is common. Reasons include:
- Fear of the abuser finding out they talked to someone
- Shame or embarrassment
- Love for the abuser (the person, not the abuse)
- Normalizing the behavior because it has been happening so long
- Fear of not being believed
- Fear of losing children, housing, or financial support

**What to do:** Do not argue or try to convince them. Simply say: *"I hear you. I'm still here if you ever want to talk."* Keep the door open.

## Part 3 — Practical Ways You Can Help

Specific, concrete offers are more useful than "let me know if you need anything."

### Safety Support

- [ ] **Be a safe contact.** Agree on a code word they can text or call you with when they need help. Keep your phone accessible.
- [ ] **Store important items.** Offer to keep copies of their important documents, a go bag, cash, or medications at your home.
- [ ] **Be the emergency contact.** Let them list you as the person to call if something happens.
- [ ] **Learn the local resources.** Have the National DV Hotline (1-800-799-7233) and local shelter number in your phone, ready to share when they are ready.
- [ ] **Help with a safety plan.** If they want help, the `templates/safety-plan.md` has a section specifically for support persons. Let them lead — you hold the information.

### Logistical Support

- [ ] **Provide transportation.** Offer rides to a shelter, court, lawyer, or doctor's appointment.
- [ ] **Offer a safe place.** If they need somewhere to go, be that place (but understand this may put you at risk — see Part 5).
- [ ] **Help with children or pets.** Offer to watch kids or care for pets during the transition period.
- [ ] **Help research resources.** Look up shelters, legal aid, and support services in your area so they don't have to do it on a monitored device.
- [ ] **Accompany them to appointments.** Offer to go with them to court, to a lawyer, or to file a protective order. Having someone there helps.

### Emotional Support

- [ ] **Check in regularly.** But be discreet — the abuser may monitor their communications.
- [ ] **Listen without trying to fix.** Sometimes they need to talk, not be told what to do.
- [ ] **Validate their feelings.** Whatever they feel — anger, love, confusion, grief — is valid.
- [ ] **Celebrate small steps.** Opening a bank account, calling a hotline, or even just talking to you — these are all progress.
- [ ] **Be patient.** This process is long and nonlinear. There may be setbacks. Stay steady.

### Workplace Support (If You Are a Coworker or Manager)

- [ ] Screen calls or visitors if needed
- [ ] Offer a flexible schedule for court dates or appointments
- [ ] Walk them to their car or transit stop
- [ ] Know your company's employee assistance program (EAP) and share it
- [ ] If you are a manager: be aware that DV can be covered under FMLA or your state's leave policies
- [ ] Report to security if the abuser has come to the workplace or made threats

## Part 4 — When Children Are Involved

If you know or suspect that children in the home are being harmed or are witnessing violence:

**Anyone can make a report.** You do not need to be a mandatory reporter.

```
Missouri Child Abuse/Neglect Hotline: 1-800-392-3738 (24/7)
Childhelp National Hotline: 1-800-422-4453 (24/7)
```

**Things to know:**
- You can report anonymously
- You do not need proof — reasonable concern is enough
- Witnessing violence IS harmful to children, even if they are not being directly hit
- Reporting does not mean the children will automatically be removed — the goal is to get the family support

**Do NOT:**
- Threaten the survivor with CPS as leverage to make them leave
- Use children as a reason to blame the survivor for staying
- Tell the abuser that you are going to report

## Part 5 — Keeping Yourself Safe

Supporting someone through abuse affects you too. Take this section seriously.

### Safety Precautions for You

- **The abuser may see you as a threat.** If the abuser knows you are helping, they may
  target you. Be aware of your own surroundings.
- **Do not confront the abuser.** Ever. This can escalate danger for everyone.
- **Be careful with digital communication.** If the abuser monitors the survivor's phone,
  your messages may be read. Use agreed-upon code words or communicate through a safe channel.
- **Document threats against you.** If the abuser threatens you, document it and consider
  contacting law enforcement.
- **Know your limits.** You are not a trained crisis counselor. It is okay to set boundaries
  about what you can and cannot do.

### Your Emotional Health

Supporting someone through abuse can cause:
- Anxiety and hypervigilance
- Sleep disruption
- Feelings of helplessness, frustration, or anger
- Compassion fatigue or burnout
- Relationship strain (with your own partner, family, or friends)
- Vicarious trauma (experiencing trauma symptoms from hearing about someone else's trauma)

**What to do:**
- [ ] **Talk to someone.** The National DV Hotline (1-800-799-7233) also supports friends and family. You can call for yourself.
- [ ] **Set boundaries.** You do not need to be available 24/7. It is okay to say: *"I care about you and I need to take a break tonight. Can we talk tomorrow?"*
- [ ] **Keep doing things you enjoy.** Do not let the crisis become your entire world.
- [ ] **Consider counseling.** A few sessions with a therapist can help you process what you are carrying.
- [ ] **Connect with others.** If there are other people supporting the survivor, coordinate so the weight is shared.
- [ ] **Recognize when you need to step back.** If this is affecting your health, relationships, or ability to function, it is okay to take a step back and redirect the survivor to professional resources.

## Part 6 — If They Go Back

Many survivors return to the abuser at least once. This can be devastating for support
people. Here is what to know:

**Why people go back:**
- The abuser promises to change (and may mean it in the moment)
- Financial dependence — they cannot afford to live on their own
- Children — they want the children to have both parents
- Love — abuse does not erase love or shared history
- Fear — the abuser threatens worse if they stay away
- Isolation — the abuser has cut them off from everyone else
- Housing — they have nowhere else to go
- Immigration — leaving may jeopardize their status
- Cultural or religious pressure to stay

**What to do:**
- **Do not give ultimatums.** ("If you go back, I'm done.") This isolates them further.
- **Keep the door open.** Say: *"I'm still here. Nothing has changed about how I feel. Whenever you're ready, I'll be here."*
- **Do not take it personally.** Their decision to return is not a rejection of your help.
- **Update safety planning.** If they go back, help them maintain a safety plan for while they are there.
- **Stay available.** The next time may be the time they leave for good.
